1|Every member of the United States Congress is required by law to disclose their stock trades. Those disclosures are public. Almost nobody reads them.
2|The STOCK Act gives them forty-five days to file. Three hundred and sixty-eight of these reports were filed this year alone, as PDFs, on a government web server, in a format designed for a filing cabinet rather than a computer. Capitol Desk reads all of them, decides whether the trade is still worth making, and executes it as an options position. Unattended.
3|The data fights you. Asset names wrap across lines. The owner column is usually blank. Twelve percent of filings are scanned paper that yields zero machine-readable text. One eighteen-page filing holds two hundred and seventy-four transactions, and extracts as nothing at all. Paper filings never print a ticker, because the form tells members not to. So Claude reads the text layer where one exists, and reads the page visually where it doesn't. And notice what else it has to do. Ignore the pre-printed example row sitting in every blank form. A naive pipeline buys Example Mega Corp.
4|Six stages, and the model only touches two of them. Claude extracts the filings and picks the contract. Everything that touches money, position sizing, risk limits, order placement, is plain arithmetic in code. Claude decides what to trade. Code decides how much. There is no prompt that gets the model a bigger position.
5|Here is a real one. Nancy Pelosi, filed the twenty-first of August. Bloom Energy calls, one hundred dollar strike, expiring June twenty twenty-seven. A deep in-the-money LEAP, used as stock replacement. But by the time we could act, Bloom Energy had already run almost fourteen percent, and that contract now costs eight thousand dollars against a five thousand dollar cap. Every affordable strike was pure time value at eighty-eight percent implied volatility. Volatility risk the filer never took. So the desk built a vertical spread instead. Long the one seventy-five, short the two thirty. Same expiry, same thesis, two thousand dollars of risk instead of eight.
6|That restraint is the whole system. Six hundred and fifty transactions parsed. Three trades placed. Six hundred and thirty-five passed over, and it says why. It declined a Procter and Gamble purchase because it recognised a routine retirement account allocation inside a family trust. It refused another because the option prices were internally incoherent. It had caught a bug in our own market data feed.
7|We measured whether any of this actually works. Eight hundred events, entering on the filing date, not the trade date, because that would be lookahead bias. Copying Congress indiscriminately does not work. Hit rates are a coin flip. The median trade is flat to negative. The positive averages come from a handful of large winners. That result argues for the design, not against it. If the average disclosure is noise, the value has to come from selection.
8|It is running now, against a real Alpaca paper account, with live positions and profit and loss. An unattended loop watches for new filings, and holds them when the market is closed rather than pricing into a session it has not seen. A review agent inspects the book through Alpaca's MCP server.
9|Anyone can build an agent that copies Nancy Pelosi. The interesting one is the agent that declines. Capitol Desk.
